My personal site sat untouched for years. The page even said so: "Hopefully, I will soon have time to continue to maintain this site."

That sentence was the most honest thing on it.

## The audit

I started with a small task: clean up the old Twitter tools page. Five links. Here is what I found:

- One returned a 404.
- One pointed back to the page it lived on.
- One domain had been taken over and now sells Instagram followers.
- One product had been renamed twice.
- One had been relaunched as something new.

Nothing had failed loudly. It had just decayed. That is how most systems fail. Not in a crash, but in a slow drift nobody owns.

## The rebuild

The fix was not a new template. It was a new operating model.

The site now lives in a git repository. Every change is versioned. Publishing is automated: merge a change and it deploys. I used an AI agent to do the heavy lifting: audit the links, move the content, rebuild the design, and wire up the pipeline. I made the decisions. It did the typing.

That split matters. The agent was fast because the direction was clear. When the direction was vague, the output was vague too. AI does not remove the need for judgment. It raises the price of not having any.

## The harder question

Once rebuilding was cheap, the real question showed up: what is this site for?

LinkedIn already carries my short-form thinking. Duplicating it here adds nothing. So this site has a different job:

- **Longer-form writing.** Ideas that need more than 300 words to hold up.
- **A more personal view.** How I think, what I'm learning, and what I do outside work.
- **A toolbox.** The tools I actually use and recommend.

## The principle

Treat your personal presence like any other system. Give it an owner, a purpose, and a way to stay current without heroics. If it depends on "when I have time," it will decay.

The next question I'm working through: what deserves the long form?
